\section{The honest frame}
\label{sec:frame}

A chain's whitepaper argues that it is safe. Its threat model argues the opposite, on
purpose, and the distance between the two is the real measure of the thing. This
document is the opposite argument. It is written the way an attacker reads a system: not
``what does it promise,'' but ``where does the promise not yet reach, and what does that
gap buy me.''

Three properties make this exercise honest rather than theatrical. First, every attack
below is rooted in a \emph{real} item — a line of code that is verifiably unguarded, or a
guard that is written but not yet enforcing — with the \texttt{file:line} quoted from the
current tree. There are no invented cryptographic breaks and no speculative attacks with
no anchor in the record. Second, each attack is graded twice: by severity (what it costs
if it lands) and by mitigation status (\mgGuard{}, \mgPartial{}, \mgOpen{}), so a reader
can tell a shipped defense from a dormant one at a glance. Third, the whole model sits
under one load-bearing caveat, stated plainly so nothing below can be read out of context.

\layman{sigilviolet}{Why a chain would publish its own attack list}{A silent weakness is
still a weakness — it just has no deadline attached. Writing it down, with the exact code
that is unguarded and how bad it would be, turns a vague risk into a debt the project owes
and can be held to. The whitepaper already opens with a security disclosure telling people
not to put real money in yet. This paper is that disclosure taken all the way down: the
full itemized attack surface, so closing it is a public obligation, not a private hope.}

\subsection*{The umbrella caveat}

\texttt{sigil-g0} is a live \emph{experimental} network. Its block-apply path is Phase-0
by design: the code comment at \texttt{sigil-node/chain.rs:84} reads ``Phase 0 omits crypto
verification,'' and \texttt{precheck()} validates byte-\emph{lengths} only, not signatures,
proof-of-work, or the verifiable-delay proof (\texttt{sigil-header:270-305},
\texttt{chain.rs:86}, \texttt{sigil-chronos lib.rs:333}). The audit states the consequence
in one sentence: \emph{``\texttt{sigil-g0} balances are not trust-bearing today.''} The
whitepaper's own security box says it out loud — \emph{``block-apply does not yet enforce
the producer's signature\ldots{} peer-sync ingress is under-authenticated\ldots{} Do not
route significant value through SIGIL until they close.''}

Every attack tree in \S\ref{sec:trees} is a \emph{specific} instance of this umbrella.
That is deliberate: naming the eight concrete gaps is what turns ``not trust-bearing yet''
from a disclaimer into a checklist.

\subsection*{Where the boundary already holds}

The honest frame cuts both ways: it must be as exact about what \emph{is} guarded as about
what is not. The RPC edge — the money-touching HTTP surface an anonymous caller can reach
on the public daemon — \emph{is} authenticated and enforcing. A per-request ed25519
wallet-signature over a domain-separated canonical message, plus a per-wallet nonce replay
store, gate every spend route (\texttt{authorize()} at
\texttt{sigil-rpc/src/bin/sigil-rpcd.rs:682}); the 2026-06-10 audit's headline finding — an
anonymous \texttt{curl} minting to the 21M cap through a difficulty-zero \texttt{/mine} —
was fixed and deployed the same day. The residual risk is therefore \emph{not} at the
edge. It is one layer down, in the consensus cryptography and the peer-sync ingress, which
no external HTTP client touches but every gossiping peer does. This paper is about that
layer.

\subsection*{How claims are graded}

SIGIL papers grade every empirical statement on two axes: an evidence grade (\stM{}
measured on the live path, \stS{} shown in seeded simulation, or claimed-by-derivation)
and a deployment grade (\stL{} live, \stD{} dormant/coded-but-not-active, or absent). A
threat model inherits the discipline exactly. An attack reproduced in a controlled
experiment is \stM{}; an attack that is the \emph{claimed consequence} of a cited, real,
unguarded item — but has not been run end-to-end — is stated as such and never dressed up
as demonstrated. A mitigation that exists as tested code behind an activation gate is
\stD{} \mgPartial{}, never \stL{} \mgGuard{}. The cardinal sin here is the category error,
not the overstatement: ``an attacker \emph{could}'' and ``an attacker \emph{did}'' are
different sentences, and this paper refuses to blur them.

\section{How to read an attack tree}
\label{sec:legend}

Each entry in \S\ref{sec:trees} is one attack, in a fixed shape. The header carries the
backlog ID, a one-line attacker goal, the \texttt{file:line} of the root primitive, a
severity marker, and a mitigation-status marker. The body rows are always the same five:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}>{\raggedleft\arraybackslash}p{2.4cm}p{12.4cm}@{}}
\toprule
{\footnotesize\textcolor{gray}{\textsc{goal}}} & what money or consensus outcome the attacker achieves\\[2pt]
{\footnotesize\textcolor{gray}{\textsc{preconditions}}} & the position, keys, or timing the attack requires\\[2pt]
{\footnotesize\textcolor{gray}{\textsc{steps}}} & how the exploit actually runs, concretely\\[2pt]
{\footnotesize\textcolor{gray}{\textsc{buys}}} & the blast radius — one wallet, the reward stream, a fork, the whole prefix\\[2pt]
{\footnotesize\textcolor{gray}{\textsc{defense}}} & the guard's true status: what is built, what is dormant, what is missing, and the height-gate strategy for a safe cutover\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent
Severity: \svC{} (consensus or full-supply), \svM{} (history, issuance, or sync),
\svMod{} (replay, reward inflation, light-client deception), \svMin{}. Mitigation:
\mgGuard{} (shipped and enforcing), \mgPartial{} (primitive built, not wired or not
enforcing), \mgOpen{} (genuinely unaddressed). Deployment/state: \stL{} the defense is
live, \stD{} the defense is dormant, \stO{} open, and \stXL{} the \emph{exploit} itself is
still live on the current path. The trees are ordered by severity, worst first.

\layman{sigilcyan}{The pattern to watch for}{Read a few entries and one shape repeats: the
\emph{fix usually already exists in the codebase} — written, unit-tested — but is switched
off, waiting behind an activation height, or has no live caller. That is not an accident of
this paper; it is the true shape of the surface, and \S\ref{sec:shape} names it. ``Built
but not wired'' is a very different risk from ``nobody has thought about it,'' and a threat
model earns its keep by telling them apart.}

\section{The attack trees}
\label{sec:trees}

\subsection{Consensus capture}

\begin{atkentry}{H1}{Forge blocks the whole network applies}{sigil-header:270-305}{\svC}{\stD~\mgPartial}
\frow{goal}{Inject arbitrary blocks that every follower applies — mint coinbase to
attacker wallets, rewrite balances, or fork the chain. Full consensus capture.}
\frow{preconditions}{A libp2p connection to a follower or producer, and the ability to
gossip a block. \emph{No key} — the producer signature field is not verified, so it need
not be valid.}
\frow{steps}{(1) Craft a block with any \texttt{producer\_sig} bytes — all-zero matches
what the honest producer already writes. (2) Pass \texttt{precheck()}, which validates
byte-lengths only. (3) Gossip it; followers apply it because the apply path runs no
producer-signature verification. (4) Embed a coinbase or \texttt{SetBalance} mutation
crediting attacker wallets.}
\frow{buys}{Everything — a single wallet up to the entire 21M cap, arbitrary forks,
follower desync. This is the highest-severity item in the backlog.}
\frow{defense}{\mgPartial{}, \stD{}. The verifier exists — \texttt{sigil-header::
verify\_producer\_sig} / \texttt{verify\_at\_height}, 7 tests green — but it is
height-gated at \texttt{H1\_PRODUCER\_SIG\_ACTIVATION\_HEIGHT = u64::MAX}, so it never
fires. Activation is blocked on three real pieces of work, all wiring not building: the
producer must actually sign (today it writes a zeroed sig); the hot path must move to
\texttt{Ed25519Hot} or gain a validator registry for the post-quantum schemes; and an
Alpha-Docker soak must pass. The activation height is the coordinated cutover: historical
zero-sig blocks keep validating under the old rule, blocks at or above the gate require a
real signature. See \texttt{docs/H1\_PRODUCER\_SIG\_GO\_NO\_GO.md}.}
\end{atkentry}

\layman{sigilred}{Why this one is the whole game}{On a finished chain, a block is only
accepted if the right producer signed it. SIGIL has written that check and tested it — but
left it switched off, because the producer isn't signing yet. Until both halves are on, a
block with a blank signature is accepted, which means someone who can talk to a node can
hand it a block that says whatever they want. This is exactly why the umbrella caveat says
not to put real money in yet: it is not a subtle flaw, it is the front door, and the paper
lists it first for that reason.}

\subsection{History substitution \& issuance}

\begin{atkentry}{M2}{Serve a malicious ``verified'' snapshot}{sigil-node/main.rs:1037}{\svM}{\stD~\mgPartial}
\frow{goal}{Feed a fast-forwarding node an attacker-chosen historical prefix that it
adopts as chain history without ever cryptographically verifying it.}
\frow{preconditions}{A node performing snapshot-pull fast-forward, and a peer position to
serve the snapshot. Latent today: snapshot-pull is gated off on the fleet default until a
signed anchor is published — but the M2 crypto gap is exactly what blocks safe activation.}
\frow{steps}{(1) Serve a snapshot whose skeleton records hash consistently — the attacker
controls the \texttt{archive\_root} computation over its own records. (2) With
\texttt{anchor\_sig} empty, nothing binds the snapshot to a trusted signer; with
\texttt{fold\_blob} empty, no fold checkpoint authenticates the prefix's chain validity.
(3) The fast-forwarding node adopts the attacker prefix.}
\frow{buys}{Wholesale history substitution for a fast-forwarding node — the largest desync
blast radius short of H1, because it rewrites the entire prefix, not one block.}
\frow{defense}{\mgPartial{}. The M1 structural layer is real: \texttt{main.rs:1030} serves
a genuine \texttt{archive\_root} (BLAKE3 over the ordered \texttt{SkeletonRecord}s, matching
the client \texttt{SnapshotVerifier}). The M2 authentication is absent —
\texttt{anchor\_sig} and \texttt{fold\_blob} are still empty (\texttt{:1037}), so
fast-forward authenticates by hash only. Two pieces close it: (a) publish a real signed
anchor — the operator pins the trust-root pubkey \texttt{SIGIL\_ANCHOR\_PK\_HEX} plus a
DNS/URL; (b) fill the trailer with a SQIsign signature and a real \texttt{flux\_fold}
checkpoint, and verify the fold on fast-forward. Because the feature is off until the
anchor exists, this is not exploitable live yet — but it cannot be safely turned on until
both land. Test gate: a wrong-signature snapshot rejected; a tampered prefix caught by
fold-verify.}
\end{atkentry}

\begin{atkentry}{C10}{Instant-mine via a forgeable VDF modulus}{flux-vdf/src/lib.rs:179}{\svM}{\stXL~\mgPartial}
\frow{goal}{Collapse the time-lane. Produce ``delayed'' verifiable-delay outputs instantly
— capture issuance, out-run honest miners, and break the timing assumption the tip-bound
challenge seed rests on.}
\frow{preconditions}{Knowledge of the \texttt{bench\_2048} modulus's factorization — it is
a benchmark modulus, so its factors are public or derivable — and a miner or
block-producer role.}
\frow{steps}{(1) Because the group order is known, compute the Wesolowski VDF output
directly instead of by sequential squaring. (2) Submit zero-real-delay ``elapsed-time''
proofs to \texttt{/mining/submit} or inside blocks. (3) Win the issuance race; the
time-lane offers no independent rate limit.}
\frow{buys}{Issuance capture — the mining reward stream and any timing-gated consensus. Not
a full-supply mint on its own, but it composes with H1.}
\frow{defense}{\mgPartial{}, and the exploit is still \stXL{}. The trustless replacement is
built: \texttt{flux-vdf/src/lib.rs:195} adds \texttt{rsa2048()} — the RSA-2048
Factoring-Challenge semiprime, unknown factorization, nothing-up-my-sleeve — plus
\texttt{production()} and \texttt{assert\_secure()} (\texttt{:226}, which rejects
small/even/prime/smooth $N$ and fails closed). But every live consensus and mining site
still calls the forgeable \texttt{bench\_2048()}: \texttt{sigil-rpcd.rs:291} (follower
apply), \texttt{:1215} (\texttt{/mining/submit}), and every miner
(\texttt{flux-miner/src/engine.rs:105,294}, \texttt{client.rs:211,231}, the miner bins).
The fix is to wire \texttt{production()} into every verify site, height-gated: pre-activation
blocks were mined against \texttt{bench\_2048}, so a clean cutover needs a height boundary
plus a coordinated miner upgrade. Small change, high blast radius — Alpha Docker first.
Test gate: a \texttt{bench\_2048}-shortcut proof rejected under the production modulus; an
honest proof verifies.}
\end{atkentry}

\begin{atkentry}{H2}{Feed sync from an unauthenticated peer}{sigil-node/src/sync\_auth.rs}{\svM}{\stD~\mgPartial}
\frow{goal}{Pose as a legitimate validator peer, serve a fabricated or forked block history
over the backfill path to a syncing node, and desync or poison its state.}
\frow{preconditions}{A libp2p connection to a node that is backfilling or bootstrapping.
While enforcement is off (the default), no valid handshake is needed.}
\frow{steps}{(1) Connect to a syncing follower. (2) Answer its \texttt{BackfillReq} with
attacker-chosen blocks; because the handshake gate is log-only by default, the
unauthenticated serve is accepted and merely telemetry-flagged. (3) Combined with H1 or H3,
deliver a fork the follower applies.}
\frow{buys}{Follower desync or fork adoption on the sync path. The target is a bootstrapping
or lagging node, not the steady-state producer.}
\frow{defense}{\mgPartial{}. The crypto is real and on-branch: \texttt{verify\_handshake} is
ed25519 over the canonical transcript, fail-closed (a Blake3 stub yields
\texttt{StubRejected}, an unwired PQ algorithm \texttt{UnsupportedAlgorithm}). The node
mints a 12-hour \texttt{ValidatorPeer} handshake at boot (key
\texttt{<db\_path>/handshake\_ed25519.key}, sync-only, not a wallet key), attaches it to
every outgoing \texttt{BackfillReq}, and the server verifies and caches sessions with
\emph{channel binding} — the \texttt{session\_pubkey} must equal the arriving libp2p peer
id, so a cross-peer replay fails inside the validity window. 11/11 crate and 42/42 node
tests. It is \stD{} because the default is log-only (authed/anon/refused telemetry) to let
the fleet migrate; \texttt{SIGIL\_HANDSHAKE\_REQUIRE=1} refuses before any block transfer.
Remaining: the \texttt{sigil-top} client attach, then the enforcement flip once telemetry
shows followers authenticate.}
\end{atkentry}

\subsection{Reward inflation, replay \& light-client deception}

\begin{atkentry}{H3}{Claim full reward for an easy share}{sigil-rpcd.rs:150}{\svMod}{\stD~\mgPartial}
\frow{goal}{Serve a block with a trivially-low difficulty, have the follower verify the
cheap proof-of-work and credit full reward — mint reward for near-zero work.}
\frow{preconditions}{A producer/peer role able to gossip a block with a chosen
\texttt{bits} field; enforcement off (the default).}
\frow{steps}{(1) Mine a block against an artificially low difficulty. (2) Declare the low
\texttt{bits} in the block; the follower verifies the share against the \emph{declared}
difficulty, not an independently reconstructed target. (3) Collect full reward for cheap
work.}
\frow{buys}{Reward inflation and mining-economics distortion; with H1, also a cheap route to
producing accepted blocks. The self-mining \texttt{/mining/submit} path was always safe —
its target is server-derived.}
\frow{defense}{\mgPartial{}. \texttt{apply\_block} now runs \texttt{check\_difficulty\_floor}
before verifying the share, enforcing the difficulty \emph{envelope}: an absolute floor
(\texttt{bits} $\geq$ \texttt{SIGIL\_MIN\_BLAKE4\_BITS}, default 10 — which kills the named
\texttt{bits=4} attack) and a per-block clamp (\texttt{bits} may not drop more than
\texttt{RETARGET\_MAX\_STEP}, 2, below the last-accepted value, valid because the producer
retargets only once per \texttt{RETARGET\_WINDOW} of 16 blocks and by at most 2). Both
bounds provably hold for honest blocks, so enforcement cannot fork off the honest chain.
Pure checker, 5/5 unit tests, telemetry \texttt{H3\_WOULD\_REJECT}; enforce with
\texttt{SIGIL\_DIFFICULTY\_ENFORCE=1}. Honest residual: the envelope \emph{bounds} the
attack but does not reconstruct the \emph{exact} required target — that needs verified
in-block timestamps and a replayable retarget rule, a consensus change that depends on the
H7 timestamp guard. The cheap-share attack is closed; a subtler in-envelope manipulation is
not yet provably excluded.}
\end{atkentry}

\begin{atkentry}{H5}{Replay a signed transaction across blocks}{sigil-tx:18-20}{\svMod}{\stD~\mgPartial}
\frow{goal}{Replay a victim's already-valid signed transaction in a later block,
re-executing the transfer to drain the sender or double-credit a recipient.}
\frow{preconditions}{Observe one valid signed transaction on the wire or in the mempool —
no key needed, the attacker replays the victim's own signature — and a path to re-inject it
into a later block.}
\frow{steps}{(1) Capture a broadcast signed transaction. (2) Re-broadcast it after it is
mined; the in-RAM mempool dedup does not span blocks or restarts. (3) The live apply path
applies it again, because no persistent per-wallet nonce high-water is consulted.}
\frow{buys}{Repeated unauthorized transfers, bounded by victim balance times replays — a
single- or few-wallet blast radius, not the whole supply.}
\frow{defense}{\mgPartial{}. The mechanism is sound and committed but has no live caller.
\texttt{sigil-state::check\_and\_bump\_nonce} (\texttt{lib.rs:336}) stores a per-wallet
high-water as a reserved \texttt{NONCE\_TOKEN} balance, so it rides the committed
\texttt{wallet\_state\_root} and a forged \texttt{SetBalance} to \texttt{NONCE\_TOKEN} is
rejected (\texttt{:882}, \texttt{ReservedToken}); \texttt{sigil-tx::apply\_signed\_batch}
(\texttt{:1360}) folds the nonce into the signed digest and rejects \texttt{nonce <= stored}
(\texttt{NonceReplay}). Both are tested. The gap: neither has any caller in
\texttt{sigil-node} or \texttt{sigil-rpcd}, so the live money path never runs them, and the
legacy \texttt{SignedTx.nonce} field is still neither signed nor checked. The fix is
wiring — route the live apply path through \texttt{apply\_signed\_batch}, or call
\texttt{check\_and\_bump\_nonce} in the RPC apply. Test gate: a replayed signed batch
rejected at the live apply path.}
\end{atkentry}

\begin{atkentry}{H4}{Fabricate a wire tip-proof}{sigil-node/src/main.rs:1686}{\svMod}{\stO~\mgOpen}
\frow{goal}{Feed a light client or peer a forged tip-proof asserting a false chain tip or
balance state; the client trusts it and answers balance queries against attacker-chosen
state.}
\frow{preconditions}{The ability to gossip on \texttt{TOPIC\_TIP\_PROOFS} to a subscribing
light client. No key required — the proof is a keyless BLAKE3 fingerprint.}
\frow{steps}{(1) Fabricate a \texttt{Blake3Fingerprint} tip-proof over any tip or state you
choose; nothing binds it to a key. (2) Publish it on the tip-proof topic. (3) A light client
with no receiver-side verification accepts it as the chain tip and serves wrong balances.}
\frow{buys}{Light-client and fresh-node deception — false balance queries, a false tip. It
does not move consensus state on full nodes, but it undermines the whitepaper's headline
fresh-node proof path.}
\frow{defense}{\mgOpen{} — genuinely unaddressed, and this one is building, not merely
wiring. The wire always builds \texttt{TipProof::new\_blake3}
(\texttt{main.rs:1686,1750}); the only \texttt{.verify()} (\texttt{:1788}) is the producer's
own dry-run self-check, not receiver ingress; \texttt{TOPIC\_TIP\_PROOFS} appears only in
the publish direction (\texttt{:1877}) with no verifying subscriber. \texttt{SqiSignBlob}
exists as a flavor (\texttt{sigil-tip-proof:52}) but \texttt{new\_sqisign} is never called
for tip-proofs. The fix is to produce \emph{and} verify \texttt{SqiSignBlob} tip-proofs on
the wire, keeping BLAKE3 as integrity-only and never a trust root — which is exactly the
contract the WS2 \texttt{/balance} verifier already gates on via
\texttt{adversary\_resistant()}. Test gate: a fabricated BLAKE3 tip-proof fails the
adversary-resistant gate; a valid SQIsign tip-proof passes.}
\end{atkentry}

\begin{atkentry}{WS2}{Prove a balance against an uncommitted root}{sigil-state roots()}{\svMod}{\stD~\mgPartial}
\frow{goal}{Serve a light client a \texttt{/balance?proof=1} response with a valid-looking
inclusion proof against a balance state the chain tip does not actually commit to.}
\frow{preconditions}{A light client relying on \texttt{/balance?proof=1}, and a node (or a
machine-in-the-middle) serving the response.}
\frow{steps}{(1) The client requests a proof-carrying balance. (2) The server returns an SMT
inclusion proof, which the client checks against an SMT root — but no \emph{tip-attested}
SMT root exists to bind that root to consensus. (3) A dishonest server presents a proof
against an SMT root the real tip never committed to; the seam is the gap between ``the proof
verifies'' and ``the root is the canonical committed root.''}
\frow{buys}{Light-client balance deception — the same victim class as H4. The proof
machinery is real; the anchoring is missing. Not a full-node consensus break.}
\frow{defense}{\mgPartial{}. The read path is realized on-branch: \texttt{prove\_balance()},
\texttt{/balance?proof=1}, and the \texttt{sigil-balance-verify} client gate on
\texttt{TipProofFlavor::adversary\_resistant()}. But the committed \texttt{wallet\_state\_root}
is still the additive accumulator, not the SMT the proof references — so the tip-proof does
not yet attest the SMT root. The fix is the height-gated Phase-3 swap the \texttt{roots()}
documentation already anticipates: add the SMT root to the header/roots, maintaining the SMT
incrementally in \texttt{set\_balance}. It depends on H4 (SQIsign tip-proofs on the wire) to
be end-to-end meaningful. Test gate: a light client trusts a \texttt{/balance?proof=1}
response only after checking it against the tip-attested SMT root.}
\end{atkentry}

\section{What we already attacked ourselves with}
\label{sec:closed}

A threat model that only lists open items reads as if the system had never been tested. It
has — adversarially, on purpose, and the results are the other half of the honest picture.
Before the DagKnight braid-ordering layer was allowed near the live network, it ran a
six-scenario adversarial simulation gate; on any state divergence a node self-halts with
\textbf{exit code 78} (no auto-restart, operator alarm) before it serves a single byte. All
six report divergence \measured{= 0}:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lp{9.4cm}l@{}}
\toprule
\textsc{scenario} & \textsc{what it attacks} & \textsc{result}\\
\midrule
S1 & 10k blocks, 3 producers, dual-instance & \measured{divergence = 0}\\
S5 & 30\% drop / reorder / equivocation & \measured{divergence = 0}\\
Orderings & 16/16 permutations agree; incremental $\equiv$ batch & \measured{16/16}\\
Withheld-block attacker & finalized prefix byte-identical & \measured{1378 blocks}\\
Tamper suite & mutated blocks must be rejected & \measured{5/5 rejected}\\
S6 & 100k blocks, maximum reorg window & \measured{258 $\leq$ 16384 bound}\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent
Corroborating live evidence: a two-producer mesh ran \measured{3385 emissions
byte-identical} before the experimental network's producer flipped to the braid binary at
height \measured{17.52M} (2026-07-02). The honesty caveat travels with the result:
\emph{simulation convergence is encouraging evidence, not a safety or liveness proof} — the
Byzantine-threshold theorem, the fold-soundness reduction, and the composition bound remain
on the open-theory rung of the evidence ladder. What the gate demonstrates is narrower and
real: across these adversaries, the ordering layer did not diverge, and where it might, it
stops rather than lies.

Three attack classes the audit once listed are now closed outright, and are recorded here so
the reader does not mistake a stale finding for a live one — the current tree wins over the
older audit on every disagreement:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Bridge mint (C9) — \mgGuard{}.} \texttt{sigil-bridge/src/lib.rs}:
\texttt{process\_deposit} (\texttt{:82}) binds amount and recipient to the proven
transaction via \texttt{deposit\_intent()} (not caller arguments), enforces a spent-set,
gates to BTC-only, and takes a PoW floor; \texttt{process\_withdrawal} (\texttt{:124})
requires the owner's ed25519 signature plus an idempotent id; \texttt{process\_ln\_deposit}
(\texttt{:146}) binds to a signed BOLT11 invoice plus spent-set. \measured{25 bridge tests}
green. The audit's ``unbounded / no replay / no sig'' is stale.
\item \textbf{Event-log root (H6) — \mgGuard{}.} \texttt{sigil-state::hash\_event\_log}
(\texttt{lib.rs:1089}) builds a positional balanced binary Merkle tree;
\texttt{sigil-events} builds the identical tree and its inclusion roundtrip is tested
(\texttt{:527}), with tamper-rejection at \texttt{:546}. The audit's ``order-blind
accumulator'' premise is factually stale. (Minor residual: event ordering is
positional-but-producer-chosen, not independently constrained.)
\item \textbf{RPC theft routes — \mgGuard{}.} The wallet-signature auth gate deployed
enforcing on the production daemon 2026-06-10 closed the anonymous mint, the owner-less
swap and liquidity spend, and the uncapped token deploy. The residual, disclosed in
\S\ref{sec:open}: the \texttt{/onboard} faucet and two demo routes.
\end{itemize}

\section{The shape of the surface}
\label{sec:shape}

Read the eight trees together and a single shape dominates. Of the eight, one is
\mgOpen{} (H4), and \emph{seven are \mgPartial{}} — the defensive primitive is written and
tested, but dormant, unwired, or enforcing only in log-only mode. This is the same shape the
Failure Atlas found across seventy-one incidents, where ``built but not wired'' was the
single largest failure class. A threat model earns its keep by naming that distinction
precisely, because the two risks demand different responses. An \mgOpen{} item needs design
and implementation; a \mgPartial{} item needs a decision, a soak, and an activation height.

The recurring instrument is the \emph{height gate}. Every consensus-touching fix here —
producer signatures (H1), the trustless VDF modulus (C10), the committed SMT root (WS2) —
lands behind an activation height rather than as an immediate rule change. This is not
timidity; it is the only way to change a validation rule on a live chain without orphaning
the history that was produced under the old rule. Pre-activation blocks keep validating by
the old rule, at-or-after blocks by the new one, and the boundary is a scheduled, revocable,
coordinated cutover. The cost is that a written defense sits visibly dormant for a while —
which is precisely the state this paper is built to disclose rather than hide.

One residual is disclosed rather than closed, because closing it fights the cold-start
problem head-on: the \texttt{/onboard} faucet still credits new wallets a fixed grant per
call, which a caller with fresh addresses can farm. A new wallet cannot pre-authenticate —
it has no funds and no history — so the honest fix is a finite, operator-funded faucet with
a per-IP rate limit and a debit against a real reserve, not an unbounded mint. Until that
ships, the faucet is a disclosed, bounded-value soft spot, not a silent one.

\layman{sigilamber}{What ``partially guarded'' really costs}{Seven of the eight weaknesses
already have their fix written and tested — it is just turned off. That is good news and a
warning at once. Good, because closing them is mostly a matter of decisions and careful
rollout, not invention. A warning, because a defense that is switched off protects nobody,
and it is easy to feel safe looking at code that never runs. This paper's job is to keep the
gap between ``the fix exists'' and ``the fix is on'' visible, dated, and owed.}

\section{Closing}
\label{sec:closing}

The distance between a whitepaper and its threat model is where a project's honesty lives.
A system that only publishes its promises asks to be trusted; a system that publishes,
grades, and dates its attack surface asks to be \emph{checked}, and gives the reader the
file and line to do it with. Every attack in this companion is a specific instance of one
disclosed sentence — that \texttt{sigil-g0} does not yet enforce its consensus cryptography
— turned into an itemized, severity-ranked, mitigation-graded list. Seven of the eight
surfaces already have their guard written and waiting behind an activation height; one is
still open. None is hidden.

That is the whole argument. An attack surface you can see is a roadmap; an attack surface
you cannot is a liability. This paper chose the first on purpose.

\vspace{8pt}
\noindent\textit{v0 — 2026-07-24. The verifier is written; the gate is at \texttt{u64::MAX};
the debt is on the ledger. A disclosed surface is a roadmap — a silent one is a liability.
Publish the attacks, grade them, date them, and let the closing of them be owed in public.}
